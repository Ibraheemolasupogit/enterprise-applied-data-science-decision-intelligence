## 1. CUPED and regression adjustment: the revenue experiment

True average effect 0.600; true pre/post correlation 0.621.

## 4. Peeking, group-sequential designs and the mSPRT

Reading: naive peeking raises the false-positive rate from 0.050 to 0.197; O'Brien–Fleming spending keeps α and nearly all of the fixed design's power (its maximum-size inflation R is 1.025) while stopping early when the effect is real; Pocock stops earliest but loses power at the same maximum size; the mSPRT tolerates continuous monitoring at a large power cost at this sample size. Under the null the naive interval at stopping under-covers, while repeated confidence intervals and confidence sequences remain valid (conservatively).

## 6. Bayesian analysis of the same experiment

The same randomised experiment analysed both ways. Economics (illustrative): conversion — 50,000 customers a year, £30 per conversion, £30,000 a year (break-even +0.020); revenue — 1,040,000 user-fortnights a year, 30% margin, £100,000 a year (break-even £0.321). Loss tolerance £10,000 a year, fixed in advance.

**Conversion** (truth 0.0100). Frequentist (M6): +0.0094, 95% confidence interval [-0.0012, 0.0200], p = 0.081; M6 rule: STOP.

| prior | posterior mean | 95% credible interval | P(effect > 0 \| data) | P(effect > break-even \| data) | E[annual value] | E[loss \| launch] | E[loss \| not launch] | decision |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| uniform Beta(1, 1) in each arm | 0.009398 | [-0.0012, 0.0200] | 0.9594 | 0.02463 | −£15,903 | £15,978 | £75 | STOP |
| sceptical: Beta centred on the historical rate, weight 5,000, both arms | 0.006267 | [-0.0023, 0.0149] | 0.9237 | 8.60e-04 | −£20,600 | £20,602 | £2 | STOP |

**Revenue** (truth 0.600). Frequentist (M6): +0.570, 95% confidence interval [0.1303, 1.0091], p = 0.0110; M6 rule: LAUNCH.

| prior | posterior mean | 95% credible interval | P(effect > 0 \| data) | P(effect > break-even \| data) | E[annual value] | E[loss \| launch] | E[loss \| not launch] | decision |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| vague N(0, 100^2) | 0.5697 | [0.1303, 1.0091] | 0.9945 | 0.8669 | £77,752 | £4,690 | £82,442 | LAUNCH |
| sceptical N(0, 0.3^2): most changes do little | 0.3656 | [0.0136, 0.7175] | 0.9791 | 0.5991 | £14,063 | £16,021 | £30,084 | HOLD |
| optimistic N(1, 0.3^2): the team's expectation | 0.7239 | [0.3719, 1.0759] | 1 | 0.9877 | £125,854 | £240 | £126,094 | LAUNCH |

Under the vague prior, 1 − P(effect > 0 | data) = 0.005520 and the one-sided p-value = 0.005520: numerically equal for a normal model with a flat prior, yet one is a probability about the effect and the other a probability about data under H0. Under the sceptical prior they differ. Conversion: the effect is probably positive (P ≈ 0.96 under the uniform prior) but almost certainly below break-even, so the expected-loss rule stops it — statistical and commercial questions differ. Revenue: the decision depends on the prior; a sceptical prior asks for more data. The prior is a pre-specified, reviewable assumption, not a tuning knob.


## 7. Bayesian rules: operating characteristics and calibration

**Frequentist properties at fixed true effects** (1000 replicates, uniform Beta priors, exact posteriors, M6 sample size for an effect of 0.015, 10 equal looks):

| rule | true effect | monitoring | launch rate [Wilson] |
|---|---|---|---|
| launch if P(better) > 0.95 | 0 | fixed horizon | 0.046 [0.035, 0.061] |
| launch if P(better) > 0.95 | 0 | checked at 10 looks | 0.153 [0.132, 0.177] |
| launch if P(better) > 0.975 | 0 | fixed horizon | 0.025 [0.017, 0.037] |
| launch if P(better) > 0.975 | 0 | checked at 10 looks | 0.088 [0.072, 0.107] |
| launch if P(better) > 0.95 | 0.015 | fixed horizon | 0.873 [0.851, 0.892] |
| launch if P(better) > 0.95 | 0.015 | checked at 10 looks | 0.924 [0.906, 0.939] |
| launch if P(better) > 0.975 | 0.015 | fixed horizon | 0.802 [0.776, 0.826] |
| launch if P(better) > 0.975 | 0.015 | checked at 10 looks | 0.869 [0.847, 0.889] |

With one analysis, 'P(better) > 0.95' launched a null change in 0.046 of experiments — under a flat prior it behaves like a one-sided 5% test. Checked at every look it launched 0.153 (3.3 times as often): posterior probabilities do not protect a repeatedly applied decision rule from optional-stopping inflation.

**Bayesian calibration** (20,000 replicates, normal model on the estimate with final SE 0.006, prior N(0, 0.01²)). Truths are drawn from a distribution; coverage averages over truths:

| scenario | 95% credible interval coverage [Wilson] | mean true effect \| launched | mean posterior mean \| launched |
|---|---|---|---|
| prior correct, analysed once | 0.949 [0.946, 0.952] | 0.01301 | 0.01295 |
| prior correct, optional stopping at P(theta > 0) > 0.95 | 0.952 [0.948, 0.954] | 0.01186 | 0.01184 |
| prior too wide (truths 3x narrower), analysed once | 0.975 [0.973, 0.977] | 0.003505 | 0.01051 |

When the prior is the true distribution of effects, credible intervals are calibrated *on average over effects* — even with optional stopping. That is a different guarantee from frequentist coverage at each fixed effect. When the prior is too wide (most real changes are smaller than assumed), launched changes are overestimated about threefold: the prior must reflect the distribution of past effects.

## 9. Switchback experiment with carryover

Effect: 4 orders per hour while on, plus carryover of 3 and 1.5 in the two hours after it is switched off; global effect 8.5. Pre-specified burn-in: 2 hours.

Without a burn-in, carryover pulls the estimate toward zero (treated periods lose it, control periods inherit it); a burn-in covering the carryover removes the bias at a modest variance cost. Treating hours as independent gets the error rate wrong in either direction depending on the design — the period is the unit.


## 10. Advanced methods against the M6 baselines

Each advanced method answers a specific question better than the M6 baseline under stated conditions; none dominates in general.

| method | M6 baseline compared with | advantage shown (this evidence) | requires | fails when | business relevance |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| CUPED / Lin adjustment | unadjusted Welch at the same n | same estimand (ITT); unbiased; SE lower by √(1 − R²) | covariate measured before assignment; R² from history for planning | covariate affected by treatment (biased); weak covariates (no gain) | 66% of the sample |
| Group-sequential (O'Brien–Fleming) | fixed horizon at the M6 size | α kept; same power with +2–3% maximum size; expected size well below the maximum when the effect is real | a few pre-planned looks; prompt outcomes; spending fixed in advance | estimates at early stopping exaggerated; unplanned extra looks need the spending function | earlier decisions for large effects |
| mSPRT / confidence sequences | fixed horizon | valid under continuous monitoring and any stopping | normal approximation with a pre-specified mixture sd | much lower power at the same maximum size | monitoring flexibility at a sample-size cost |
| Bayesian expected loss | M6 LAUNCH/HOLD/STOP at fixed horizon | decision in money with explicit priors | prior reflects past effects; model fits | frequentist error rates not controlled when peeking; prior misspecification biases launched effects | communicates risk in £; not a replacement for pre-registration |
| Cluster planning with estimated ICC | plan ignoring clustering | planned power achieved | ICC estimable from history; clusters exchangeable | few clusters; ICC uncertainty | avoids an underpowered rollout |
| Switchback with burn-in, blocked | naive hourly comparison | unbiased global effect; valid randomisation inference | carryover shorter than the burn-in; no anticipation | carryover longer than the burn-in; few periods | measures market-level effects user-level tests cannot |
