## 1. Causal DAG of the M2 campaign and identification

Isolated: acquisition_channel, region.

**Reading this table honestly.** The naive contrast (0.226) is almost three times the true effect: treated customers have higher `latent_value` and would have retained better anyway. Valid adjustment (regime A, regime C) removes most of that bias, but on this particular seed the valid estimates land 0.046–0.056 above the truth and all but one of their 95% intervals narrowly miss 0.08 (g-computation adjusting for latent_value alone just covers it). That is what a ~2-standard-error draw looks like; whether the estimators are biased cannot be judged from one dataset. Section 7 answers it with 200 independent M2 draws (no detectable bias; near-nominal coverage). Conversely, both *invalid* post-treatment strategies produce intervals that cover 0.08 on this seed, although section 7 shows they are biased by −0.044 and −0.024 on average — a single good-looking number is not validation, and only the DAG argument tells valid from invalid here.

## 4. Negative-control outcome

`log_income` is measured before the campaign, so the offer cannot change it (true effect 0), but it shares the confounder `latent_value`. A non-zero adjusted 'effect' on it signals residual confounding.

| analysis | estimate | 95% CI | covers 0 |
|---|---|---|---|
| negative control (log income), naive | 0.08797 | [0.054, 0.122] | no |
| negative control (log income), regime A | -0.03795 | [-0.073, -0.003] | no |
| negative control (log income), regime B | 0.009802 | [-0.025, 0.044] | yes |

The naive contrast detects the confounding clearly. On this seed the regime A estimate is ≈ 2 SE below zero — the same kind of chance draw as in section 2 (across replicates it is unbiased with near-nominal coverage; section 7). Regime B's interval happens to cover 0 here, but section 7 shows its negative-control estimate is biased by about +0.05 on average and flags the residual confounding in roughly 80% of draws — this seed is one of the misses. A single negative-control check is informative but not conclusive in either direction.

## 5. Conditional (subgroup) effects

AIPW within each segment, adjusting for latent_value. The true conditional effect is 0.08 in **every** segment (constant on the risk-difference scale).

| segment | estimate | 95% CI | covers 0.08 |
|---|---|---|---|
| casual | 0.09449 | [0.025, 0.164] | yes |
| power | 0.1944 | [0.065, 0.324] | yes |
| regular | 0.1566 | [0.083, 0.230] | no |

Heterogeneity test: Q = 2.46 on 2 df, p = 0.292; inverse-variance pooled effect 0.133. The apparent spread between segments is compatible with noise — exactly the trap of reading subgroup point estimates as heterogeneity. A constant risk difference also implies *non-constant* risk ratios and odds ratios across segments with different baselines: whether an effect is 'heterogeneous' depends on the scale.

## 6. Sensitivity to unmeasured confounding

Regime B (proxies only) is the realistic case: the confounder is unmeasured.

AIPW risk ratio 1.290 (95% CI 1.195–1.392); marginal risks 0.676 vs 0.524. **E-value 1.90** (CI limit: 1.68): an unmeasured confounder associated with both treatment and retention by a risk ratio of 1.90 each, beyond the proxies, could explain the whole estimate.

**Benchmark against the real omitted confounder** (possible only because the data are synthetic), using the Ding & VanderWeele (2016) bounding factor B = RR_EU·RR_UD / (RR_EU + RR_UD − 1): the most a confounder with prevalence ratio RR_EU between arms and outcome risk ratio RR_UD could inflate the observed RR.

| latent_value categories | RR_EU (prevalence ratio) | RR_UD (max/min retention) | bounding factor B | RR could fall to |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 1.983 | 1.403 | 1.166 | 1.106 |
| 4 | 3.647 | 1.699 | 1.426 | 0.9046 |
| 10 | 8.54 | 2.089 | 1.853 | 0.6962 |

A coarse median split understates a continuous confounder: with finer categories the bound reaches 1.85, above the observed RR of 1.29. So a confounder as strong as the one regime B omits *could*, in the worst case the bound describes, explain away the whole estimate — the E-value analysis correctly flags the regime-B estimate as not robust. The actual bias is smaller than that worst case (the effect is real, 0.08, and regime B overstates it by about a third on average, section 7): the bound is conservative by design. Caveats: these are marginal associations whereas the E-value refers to associations conditional on the measured proxies, and with many small bins the max/min ratios are inflated by sampling noise. The E-value says how much unmeasured confounding an estimate can withstand, not whether it is right.

| negative control (log income), AIPW regime B | 0 | 0.0484 | +0.0484 ± 0.0012 | 0.01688 | 0.01712 | 0.05124 | 0.200 [0.150, 0.261] | 1.83e-167 |

**Interpretation.** Valid strategies (regime A, regime C, both single-misspecification AIPW variants) show no bias beyond Monte Carlo error. Their coverage ranges 0.930–0.940; every Wilson interval contains 0.95 and the smallest exact binomial p-value against 0.95 is 0.19, so none is statistically distinguishable from nominal. The converse also holds: 200 replications cannot rule out true coverage a few points below 95% (the intervals reach down to ≈ 0.89). Reported SEs track the empirical SDs; IPW SEs include the correction for propensity estimation, and AIPW's fixed-nuisance SEs remain close even under single-model misspecification in this design. The naive contrast is biased by more than the effect itself. Observed proxies (regime B) remove most of the bias but not all, and their intervals under-cover. Adjusting for a post-treatment variable, or selecting on one, biases the estimate *downwards*. The negative control behaves as the DAG predicts: centred on 0 with valid adjustment, shifted under proxy adjustment.

Valid analyses have mean errors within about 3 Monte Carlo SEs of zero; the failures are biased by tens to hundreds of Monte Carlo SEs.

**Positivity** (x ~ U(−2, 2), e = sigmoid(s·x), effect 1 + x²; 100 replications, n = 2,000). Trimming keeps units with estimated 0.1 ≤ e ≤ 0.9.

| strength s | median min-arm ESS share | IPW RMSE (vs ATE) | AIPW RMSE (vs ATE) | trimmed AIPW mean error | trimmed AIPW RMSE |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.6969 | 0.07155 | 0.08262 | -0.005706 | 0.07875 |
| 3 | 0.05591 | 0.2646 | 0.7018 | -0.01156 | 0.09352 |
| 6 | 0.02848 | 0.85 | 9.708 | 0.009674 | 0.1378 |

The trimmed estimate is scored against the true effect among the units actually kept (trimming uses *estimated* propensities). Under poor overlap (s ≥ 3) AIPW does worse than IPW because its arm-specific outcome models are linear in x while the treated outcome is quadratic (effect 1 + x²): with a misspecified outcome model AIPW leans on the weights, and the weights are extreme. As overlap deteriorates the effective sample collapses and both IPW and AIPW estimates of the population ATE degrade; trimming restores precision for a *different* estimand (the effect where both treatments occur), which here is smaller than the ATE because the effect is largest where positivity fails.

**Double robustness** (Kang & Schafer-style design, effect 10; 200 replications, n = 1,000; 'wrong' = model in non-linear transforms of the confounders).

| propensity model | outcome model | mean | median | SD | RMSE |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| correct | correct | 10 | 9.997 | 0.07872 | 0.07853 |
| correct | wrong | 10.02 | 9.858 | 1.674 | 1.67 |
| wrong | correct | 9.993 | 9.992 | 0.2441 | 0.2436 |
| wrong | wrong | -4.136 | 1.769 | 22.56 | 26.58 |

AIPW is consistent when either model is right, but with a wrong propensity model it inherits heavy tails from extreme weights (SD and occasional outliers). With both wrong it is far worse than either single-model estimator — double robustness is not a licence for careless modelling.
