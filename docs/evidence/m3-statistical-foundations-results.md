## 1. Randomised experiment: conversion lift (M2 data)

**Question.** Did the recommendation feature raise 14-day conversion? Assignment was randomised (M2 `experiments.py`), so the difference in proportions estimates the average treatment effect on conversion for the eligible population.

Arms: control n = 566, treatment n = 569.

| quantity | estimate | 95% lower | 95% upper | method |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| control conversion | 0.106 | 0.08325 | 0.1341 | Wilson |
| treatment conversion | 0.1371 | 0.1112 | 0.1678 | Wilson |
| risk difference | 0.03108 | -0.007075 | 0.06929 | Newcombe hybrid score |
| risk difference | 0.03108 | -0.005971 | 0.06812 | stratified percentile bootstrap (2,000) |
| risk ratio | 1.293 | 0.943 | 1.773 | Katz log |
| odds ratio | 1.34 | 0.9359 | 1.918 | Woolf log |

Pooled two-proportion z = 1.602, two-sided p = 0.109; Cohen's h = 0.095.

**Design check (not post-hoc power).** For the pre-specified minimum effect of 0.04 (absolute) at the observed control rate, these arm sizes give power 0.53; 80% power needs about 1080 customers per arm.

**Known-truth check (evaluation only).** The design value is 0.04; the 95% interval covers it.

**Interpretation.** The point estimate is close to the true effect, but the interval includes zero: this experiment is underpowered for an effect of this size. 'Not significant' here means 'not enough data', not 'no effect' — the correct decision-relevant output is the interval, and the design check shows the sample needed. (Experimentation design and sequential decision rules are M6/M7 scope.)

## 4. Dependence: clustered transactions (M2 data)

**Question.** How strongly are transaction amounts associated with customer segment and price sensitivity — and how uncertain is that, given each customer contributes many transactions?

6151 transactions from 1478 customers.

| feature | coefficient | classical SE | HC3 SE | cluster (customer) SE | cluster / classical |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| intercept | 9.658 | 0.1854 | 0.1813 | 0.4218 | 2.275 |
| segment_regular | -0.2854 | 0.1023 | 0.0838 | 0.1935 | 1.892 |
| segment_power | 0.5715 | 0.1685 | 0.147 | 0.3583 | 2.126 |
| price_sensitivity | -4.259 | 0.2664 | 0.2618 | 0.6113 | 2.294 |

**Interpretation.** Point estimates are identical across estimators; only the uncertainty differs. Transactions of one customer share a plan price, so errors are strongly correlated within customer and the effective sample size is closer to the number of customers than the number of rows. Classical and HC3 SEs (which assume independent rows) understate uncertainty by a factor of about two; reporting them would overstate precision. The repeated-sampling study below confirms the consequence for interval coverage.

## 5. Missing income: known-truth check (M2 data)

**Question.** Can mean log household income be estimated from a column with missing values?

| estimate | mean log income | error vs full data |
|---|---|---|
| full data (twin build, no injected missingness) | 10.77 | — |
| M2 complete case (MCAR, 5.5% missing) | 10.78 | 0.002215 |
| imposed MAR complete case (30.3% missing) | 10.73 | -0.04092 |
| imposed MAR, regression imputation on price sensitivity | 10.76 | -0.01087 |

Standard error of the M2 complete-case mean: 0.0087. Correlation of log income with price sensitivity: -0.262.

**Interpretation.** M2 injects missingness completely at random, so dropping the missing rows costs precision but not validity (error well within one SE). When missingness instead depends on an observed covariate associated with income (MAR), the complete-case mean is biased by several standard errors; regressing on that covariate removes most of the bias. Whether real missingness is MCAR, MAR or MNAR cannot be tested from the observed data — this check is possible only because the data are synthetic.

## 6. Type I error under H0 (simulation)

Rejection rate at α = 0.05 when H0 is true (Monte Carlo SE in brackets).

| test | data-generating process (H0 true) | replications | type I error |
|---|---|---|---|
| Welch t | N(0,3²), n=10 vs N(0,1), n=40 | 10000 | 0.052 (± 0.002) |
| Student pooled t | N(0,3²), n=10 vs N(0,1), n=40 | 10000 | 0.256 (± 0.004) |
| Welch t | N(0,1), n=20 vs N(0,1), n=20 | 10000 | 0.051 (± 0.002) |
| Mann–Whitney U | Exp(1), n=15 vs Exp(1), n=15 | 10000 | 0.046 (± 0.002) |
| two-proportion z | Bin(500, 0.2) vs Bin(500, 0.2) | 10000 | 0.050 (± 0.002) |

**Interpretation.** Welch's test holds its level under unequal variances; Student's pooled test does not when the smaller group has the larger variance — the reason Welch is the default. The exact Mann–Whitney test is slightly conservative (discrete null distribution).

## 7. Confidence-interval coverage (simulation)

Proportion of nominal 95% intervals containing the true parameter.

| interval | data-generating process | replications | coverage |
|---|---|---|---|
| t interval | N(5, 2²), n=12 | 10000 | 0.950 (± 0.002) |
| t interval | LogNormal(0, 1.5), n=10 | 10000 | 0.718 (± 0.005) |
| t interval | LogNormal(0, 1.5), n=200 | 10000 | 0.883 (± 0.003) |
| percentile bootstrap | Exp(mean 2), n=20 | 1000 | 0.896 (± 0.010) |
| BCa bootstrap | Exp(mean 2), n=20 | 1000 | 0.906 (± 0.009) |
| t interval | Exp(mean 2), n=20 | 10000 | 0.913 (± 0.003) |
| Wilson | Bin(40, 0.05) | 10000 | 0.950 (± 0.002) |
| Wald | Bin(40, 0.05) | 10000 | 0.870 (± 0.003) |

**Interpretation.** The t interval is exact for normal data. For heavy-tailed data it under-covers badly at small n and improves only slowly with n — log-normal (σ = 1.5) data still give well under 95% at n = 200, because the sample mean's skewness decays like 1/√n (section 9). The bootstrap does not rescue small samples: at n = 20 from an exponential population both bootstrap intervals under-cover, BCa slightly less than percentile, and neither beats the plain t interval here. Wald binomial intervals fail near 0; Wilson intervals hold their level.

## 9. Central Limit Theorem diagnostics (simulation)

Sampling distribution of the mean (5,000 replications per n).

| population | n | SD of means | σ/√n | skewness of means | theory γ/√n | KS distance to N(0,1) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Exponential(1) | 2 | 0.7139 | 0.7071 | 1.507 | 1.414 | 0.09947 |
| Exponential(1) | 10 | 0.3181 | 0.3162 | 0.6377 | 0.6325 | 0.04489 |
| Exponential(1) | 30 | 0.1841 | 0.1826 | 0.3371 | 0.3651 | 0.02629 |
| Exponential(1) | 100 | 0.1014 | 0.1 | 0.179 | 0.2 | 0.01535 |
| Exponential(1) | 1000 | 0.03174 | 0.03162 | 0.04538 | 0.06325 | 0.01678 |
| LogNormal(0, 1) | 2 | 1.463 | 1.528 | 3.678 | 4.373 | 0.1653 |
| LogNormal(0, 1) | 10 | 0.6637 | 0.6834 | 1.389 | 1.956 | 0.09451 |
| LogNormal(0, 1) | 30 | 0.3889 | 0.3946 | 0.9306 | 1.129 | 0.0579 |
| LogNormal(0, 1) | 100 | 0.2177 | 0.2161 | 0.7119 | 0.6185 | 0.03888 |
| LogNormal(0, 1) | 1000 | 0.06799 | 0.06834 | 0.1594 | 0.1956 | 0.02098 |

**Interpretation.** The standard error is σ/√n at every n — that part of the theory is exact. Normality is the asymptotic part: skewness decays like γ/√n, so the log-normal population (γ ≈ 6.2) needs far larger samples than the exponential (γ = 2) before normal-theory inference is accurate. 'n ≥ 30' is not a universal rule. Simulation noise: with 5,000 replications the KS distance cannot fall much below ≈0.012 even for an exactly normal statistic, and sample skewness has a Monte Carlo SE of ≈0.035 (larger for heavy-tailed populations), so differences at n = 100 vs 1,000 are within noise.

## 10. Bias–variance decomposition (simulation)

f(x) = sin(2.5x), x ~ U(−1, 1), σ = 0.3, n = 30 training points, 1,000 training sets; test grid on [−0.9, 0.9].

| model | bias² | variance | noise σ² | sum | simulated test MSE | mean training MSE |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| polynomial degree 1 | 0.05913 | 0.0126 | 0.09 | 0.1617 | 0.1602 | 0.1524 |
| polynomial degree 3 | 4.42e-04 | 0.0123 | 0.09 | 0.1027 | 0.1014 | 0.07825 |
| polynomial degree 5 | 1.08e-05 | 0.02744 | 0.09 | 0.1175 | 0.1149 | 0.0717 |
| polynomial degree 8 | 7.86e-04 | 0.3955 | 0.09 | 0.4863 | 0.486 | 0.0627 |
| ridge degree 8, λ=0.01 | 6.41e-05 | 0.1406 | 0.09 | 0.2307 | 0.2269 | 0.06727 |
| ridge degree 8, λ=1 | 0.01172 | 0.0288 | 0.09 | 0.1305 | 0.1283 | 0.08518 |
| ridge degree 8, λ=10 | 0.09787 | 0.02429 | 0.09 | 0.2122 | 0.2115 | 0.1696 |

**Interpretation.** Test error is U-shaped in complexity while training error falls monotonically — training error cannot select complexity. The simulated test MSE matches bias² + variance + σ² up to Monte Carlo error, an independent check of the decomposition. Ridge reaches a similar trade-off continuously at fixed degree. Variance is not strictly monotone in complexity under a random design: the misspecified straight line also varies with where training points fall.

## 11. Missing-data strategies by mechanism (simulation)

(X, Y) bivariate normal, ρ = 0.6, n = 200, 40% of Y missing, target E[Y] = 0; 2,000 replications. Naive SE = sd/√(rows analysed).

| mechanism | strategy | bias | MC SE of bias | naive SE / true SD | 95% CI coverage |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| MCAR | complete case | 0.001476 | 0.002076 | 0.9833 | 0.942 |
| MCAR | mean imputation | 0.001476 | 0.002076 | 0.5892 | 0.75 |
| MCAR | regression imputation | -6.67e-05 | 0.001879 | 0.7265 | 0.859 |
| MCAR | stochastic regression | 5.88e-04 | 0.002059 | 0.767 | 0.8695 |
| MAR | complete case | -0.2564 | 0.002014 | 0.9634 | 0.1685 |
| MAR | mean imputation | -0.2564 | 0.002014 | 0.5762 | 0.0415 |
| MAR | regression imputation | -1.97e-04 | 0.002075 | 0.6564 | 0.7995 |
| MAR | stochastic regression | 3.95e-04 | 0.002232 | 0.7074 | 0.833 |
| MNAR | complete case | -0.4256 | 0.00193 | 0.9079 | 5.00e-04 |
| MNAR | mean imputation | -0.4256 | 0.00193 | 0.5442 | 0 |
| MNAR | regression imputation | -0.3001 | 0.001872 | 0.628 | 0.0105 |
| MNAR | stochastic regression | -0.2994 | 0.001983 | 0.6948 | 0.0275 |

**Interpretation.** Under MCAR, complete-case analysis is unbiased and its SE honest. Under MAR it is biased; conditioning on the observed driver (regression imputation) removes the bias, but single imputation treats imputed values as data, so naive SEs are too small and coverage falls. Mean imputation is the worst of both: biased under MAR and overconfident everywhere. Under MNAR no strategy based only on observed data is unbiased.
