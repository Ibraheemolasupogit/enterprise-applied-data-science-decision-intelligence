# Advanced experimentation (Milestone 7)

Status: `IMPLEMENTED`, `TESTED`, `SIMULATED`, `BENCHMARKED` as of Milestone 7 — see
[../evidence/capability-status.md](../evidence/capability-status.md). Numerical evidence:
[../evidence/m7-advanced-experimentation-results.md](../evidence/m7-advanced-experimentation-results.md)
(generated by `python scripts/generate_m7_evidence.py`, about three minutes). Section references
("results §n") point to that file.

M6 analyses an experiment once, at a planned sample size, by comparing arms as randomised. M7
extends that baseline in four directions, each answering a *different* question. They are not
interchangeable upgrades (Brief §7.4):

| Method | Question it answers | Compared with (M6 baseline) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| CUPED / regression adjustment | Can the same experiment be decisive with fewer users? | unadjusted Welch difference at the same n |
| Group-sequential designs | Can we look at the data before the end without inflating false positives? | one fixed-horizon test |
| Always-valid inference (mSPRT) | Can we monitor continuously and stop at any time? | one fixed-horizon test |
| Bayesian expected loss | What is the probability and money at risk of each decision, given a stated prior? | M6 LAUNCH/HOLD/STOP rule |
| Cluster-randomised planning | How many cities/stores do we need when outcomes are correlated within them? | plan that ignores clustering |
| Switchback | What is the market-level effect when users interact through a shared market? | naive hour-level comparison |

## Scope and boundaries

In scope: CUPED and Lin regression adjustment with pre-treatment covariates and leakage checks;
Lan–DeMets and classical group-sequential boundaries, operating characteristics, repeated
confidence intervals and monitoring of observed information; the normal-mixture SPRT, always-valid
p-values and confidence sequences; Beta-binomial and normal-normal Bayesian analysis with explicit
priors and expected-loss decisions; ICC estimation and cluster-randomised planning; switchback
designs with burn-in, time-of-day blocking and randomisation inference; an additive scenario
generator (`data/advanced_experiment_scenarios.py`).

Out of scope (not implemented): geo experiments, interference and network exposure models,
heterogeneous-effect (CATE) analysis, multi-armed bandits, long-term/surrogate effect estimation,
experiment-interaction detection, bias-adjusted estimation after group-sequential stopping, and
futility boundaries. Bandits in particular answer a different question (cumulative reward under
adaptive allocation) and would not replace a controlled comparison.

## 1. CUPED and regression adjustment — `experimentation/variance_reduction.py`

| Item | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Question | Did the change raise 14-day revenue per user, estimated as precisely as the data allow? |
| Estimand | Unchanged from M6: ITT average effect of assignment. Adjustment changes precision, never the target. |
| Data assumptions | Covariate `X` measured before assignment (unaffected by treatment); independent units; randomised assignment. |
| Formulation | CUPED (Deng et al. 2013): `Y_adj = Y − (X − X̄)'θ`, `θ` = pooled OLS slope of `Y` on `X`; Welch difference of `Y_adj` (M6). Lin (2013): OLS of `Y` on `Z`, centred `X`, `Z × X`; HC2 SE (M3 OLS). Variance factor `1 − R²`. Missing pre-period values (new users): mean imputation + indicator — pre-treatment, so valid. |
| Baseline | M6 unadjusted Welch on the same data. |
| Evaluation | Case (results §1): CUPED 0.581 [0.227, 0.934] vs unadjusted 0.394 [−0.045, 0.834], truth 0.600; variance −35.3%. Repeated sampling (§2, 400 populations): bias −0.011 ± 0.015 (CUPED) vs −0.028 ± 0.017 (unadjusted); coverage 0.953 for both; power 0.487 vs 0.340; A/A 0.048 for both; realised variance reduction 0.339 = mean pooled R² 0.339. |
| Reference checks | CUPED equals the manual formula; Lin's estimate and HC2 SE equal statsmodels OLS `HC2`; variance reduction equals `ρ²` on large samples (tests). |
| Failure modes | A covariate measured after assignment (in-experiment sessions) biases the estimate by −2.79 ± 0.01 and *looks* like a better variance reducer (46% vs 35%); a "pre-period" window overlapping the experiment by 7 days biases it by −0.114 ± 0.013. A weak covariate (binary pre-period conversion) reduces variance by only 2.6%. |
| Guard | `check_adjustment_covariate` FAILs any covariate whose window ends after assignment; the arm difference is reported as supporting evidence only (a non-significant difference does not make a post-assignment covariate safe). |
| Decision relevance | With R² = 0.336 estimated on a *historical* cohort, the same power needs 66% of the users: 75 instead of 112 days (§1). On the case data the M6 rule moves from HOLD (unadjusted) to LAUNCH (CUPED) — the same estimand, a smaller standard error. |

CUPED's single pooled slope and Lin's interacted regression agree to 2e-4 here. Lin's estimator is
never less efficient than the unadjusted estimator asymptotically even with heterogeneous effects
and unequal allocation; CUPED can be marginally less efficient when effects vary with `X`. Both
SEs treat estimated coefficients as fixed — exact asymptotically, slightly optimistic in very small
samples.

## 2. Sequential experimentation — `experimentation/sequential.py`

**Why.** A fixed-horizon test is valid only if performed once. Testing at 5, 10 or 20 equally
spaced looks and stopping at the first p ≤ 0.05 raises the type I error to 0.142, 0.193 and 0.248
(Armitage, McPherson & Rowe 1969; reproduced exactly, results §3; simulated 0.197 for 10 looks,
§4).

| Item | Group-sequential (Lan–DeMets spending) | Always-valid (mSPRT) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Question | Can we stop early at a few pre-planned looks? | Can we monitor continuously and stop any time? |
| Estimand | ITT difference, as M6 | ITT difference, as M6 |
| Assumptions | `Z_k` approximately normal with independent increments (CLT for i.i.d. units); outcomes observed promptly or analysed only when complete; spending function fixed in advance | Same normal approximation; mixture sd `τ` fixed in advance |
| Formulation | Boundaries `c_k` with `P(|Z_j| ≥ c_j for some j ≤ k | H0) = α(t_k)`, computed by recursive numerical integration (Jennison & Turnbull 2000, ch. 19) on a Simpson grid | `Λ_n = √(V/(V+τ²)) exp(τ² θ̂² / (2V(V+τ²)))`, a martingale under H0; reject when `Λ_n ≥ 1/α` (Ville's inequality) |
| Inference | Repeated confidence intervals `θ̂_k ± c_k SE_k` (simultaneous coverage ≥ 1 − α) | Confidence sequence `{θ: Λ_n(θ) < 1/α}` and always-valid p-values |
| Baseline | M6 fixed-horizon test at the M6 sample size | same |

Reference checks (results §3, tests): Pocock and O'Brien–Fleming constants for K = 2, 3, 4, 5, 10
match Jennison & Turnbull Table 2.3 to 3 dp; Lan–DeMets O'Brien–Fleming boundaries for five looks
match gsDesign/rpact/ldbounds (4.877, 3.357, 2.680, 2.290, 2.031); maximum-sample inflation
R = 1.228 (Pocock) and 1.028 (O'Brien–Fleming) match Tables 2.2/2.4; size and power agree with
SciPy's multivariate-normal integration to within its own error (about 1e-7; tested at 1e-6)
and with simulation; the mSPRT statistic equals a
quadrature of the mixture likelihood ratio, and its confidence sequence inverts the test exactly.

**Convention.** The Lan–DeMets O'Brien–Fleming function is applied per side at α/2, as in
gsDesign, rpact and ldbounds. The alternative two-sided form spends more early (first boundary 4.38
for five looks) and is documented but not used.

Operating characteristics (results §4; 4,000 replicates per effect, 10 looks, conversion 12%,
maximum = the M6 size for +0.015):

| Procedure | Size | Power at +0.015 | Expected sample (fraction) at +0.015 / +0.030 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fixed horizon (M6) | 0.050 | 0.803 | 1 / 1 |
| Naive peeking (invalid) | 0.197 | 0.869 | 0.50 / 0.21 |
| Lan–DeMets O'Brien–Fleming | 0.051 | 0.789 | 0.77 / 0.45 |
| Lan–DeMets Pocock | 0.049 | 0.705 | 0.69 / 0.30 |
| mSPRT, checked 100 times | 0.015 | 0.527 | 0.78 / 0.35 |

**Inference after stopping.** Among experiments that stop and reject at +0.015, the mean estimate is
0.0191 (O'Brien–Fleming), 0.0231 (Pocock), 0.0245 (mSPRT), against 0.0169 for the fixed-horizon
test among its rejections: conditioning on significance alone exaggerates (the significance filter),
and early stopping adds to it.
Repeated confidence intervals (0.977 coverage) and confidence sequences (0.998) remain valid; the
naive interval at stopping under peeking covers 0.803 under the null. Business projections from an
early-stopped experiment should use the interval, not the point estimate. Bias-adjusted estimators
(e.g. median-unbiased estimation by stage-wise ordering) are not implemented.

**Monitoring in practice** (results §5). With a 14-day outcome, a look on day *d* analyses only
users who arrived by *d* − 14; boundaries are recomputed for the observed information fractions.
In the case study the CUPED-adjusted O'Brien–Fleming design stops on day 28 instead of 42
(Z = 3.37 ≥ 2.96), with repeated 95% CI [0.106, 1.636] covering the truth 0.600. The naive p-value
was already 0.048 on day 21, where the boundary was 4.33.

## 3. Bayesian A/B analysis — `experimentation/bayesian.py`

| Item | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Question | Given the data *and a stated prior*, how probable is each effect, and what is the expected monetary loss of each decision? |
| Estimand | The effect θ (difference in conversion or mean revenue) as a random quantity with a posterior distribution. |
| Model | Conversion: independent `Beta(a, b)` priors, exact conjugate posteriors; summaries by one-dimensional quadrature (no Monte Carlo). Revenue: normal prior on θ with the normal likelihood of the M6 estimate (CLT; SE treated as known). |
| Outputs | Posterior mean; equal-tailed **credible** interval; `P(θ > 0 | data)`; `P(θ > break-even | data)`; expected loss of launching `E[max(−V, 0)]` and of not launching `E[max(V, 0)]`, `V = θ × scale − cost`. |
| Decision | LAUNCH if E[loss | launch] ≤ tolerance; STOP if E[loss | not launch] ≤ tolerance; else HOLD. Tolerance (£10,000/year) and economics fixed in advance. |
| Baseline | M6 frequentist analysis and decision rule on the same data. |
| Reference checks | `P(p_t > p_c)` equals the closed form (Miller 2015) to 1e-8 for 40–100,000 users per arm; credible intervals, tail probabilities and expected losses match 1M-draw Monte Carlo; the normal posterior matches grid integration of prior × likelihood (tests). |

**Credible interval ≠ confidence interval; posterior probability ≠ p-value.** A credible interval
contains θ with posterior probability 0.95 *given the prior*; a confidence interval is a procedure
covering a fixed θ in 95% of repetitions. With a vague prior and a normal model they coincide
numerically: 1 − P(θ > 0 | data) = 0.005520 = the one-sided p-value (results §6). Under a sceptical
prior they do not, and the meaning never coincides.

**Case (results §6).** Conversion: P(θ > 0) = 0.96 but P(θ > break-even 0.02) = 0.025 — almost
surely positive, almost surely not worth its cost; expected-loss rule STOP (M6: STOP). Revenue: the
decision depends on the prior — vague LAUNCH, sceptical N(0, 0.3²) HOLD, optimistic N(1, 0.3²)
LAUNCH (M6: LAUNCH). Priors are pre-specified, reviewable assumptions; the evidence reports all
three rather than choosing one after seeing the data.

**Frequentist behaviour (results §7).** At a fixed effect of zero, "launch if P(better) > 0.95"
analysed once launched 0.046 [0.035, 0.061] of null changes — a one-sided 5% test under a flat prior;
applied at each of 10 looks it launched 0.153 [0.132, 0.177], 3.3 times as often (1,000 replicates). Posterior probabilities do not protect a repeatedly
applied decision rule. **Bayesian calibration** is a different guarantee: when the truths are drawn
from the prior, 95% credible intervals cover 0.949 analysed once and 0.952 with optional stopping
(20,000 replicates); when the prior is three times too wide, launched effects are overestimated
threefold (posterior mean 0.0105 vs true 0.0035).

## 4. Cluster-randomised planning — `experimentation/designs.py`

| Item | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Question | How many cities must be randomised to detect the target effect? |
| Units | Randomisation unit: city. Analysis: CR1 with t(G − 1) or cluster means (M6). Users are measurement units, not independent replicates. |
| Formulation | ICC by one-way ANOVA (unbalanced `n0`), cluster-bootstrap CI (M3 bootstrap); design effect `1 + ((cv² + 1)m − 1) ICC` (M6); clusters per arm = smallest G with approximate power (effective n = G m / DE, t(2G − 1) critical value) ≥ target. |
| Data | ICC estimated on a *historical* cohort (the experiment's own outcomes contain the effect). |
| Evaluation (results §8) | ICC 0.0126 [0.0079, 0.0176]; DE 2.26 with 100 users per city; 127 cities per arm planned vs 57 if the ICC is ignored. Simulated CR1 power: 0.802 [0.761, 0.839] planned 0.803; naive plan 0.482 (planned 0.464). Planned power across the ICC CI: 0.72–0.89. |
| Reference checks | ICC equals the F-statistic identity `(F − 1)/(F + n0 − 1)` with SciPy's ANOVA; consistency on simulated data; with ICC = 0 the planned power equals the individual-randomisation power (tests). |
| Failure modes | Few clusters (t(G − 1) and CR1 become unreliable; M5 wild cluster bootstrap); ICC uncertainty (plan on the upper bound when the cost of an underpowered rollout is high); cluster sizes correlated with effects (cluster-weighted vs unit-weighted estimands differ, M6). |

## 5. Switchback experiments — `experimentation/designs.py`

| Item | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Question | What is the effect of a marketplace-wide pricing change when user-level randomisation would violate SUTVA (shared drivers, inventory, prices)? |
| Estimand | Global effect: mean orders per hour with the change always on minus always off (direct + carryover). |
| Design | 14 days of 4-hour periods (84), randomised within time-of-day blocks (M6 stratified randomisation): 7 treated and 7 control periods per block. |
| Analysis | Period means after a pre-specified burn-in (2 hours); M6 `stratified_difference` (Neyman variance); randomisation p-value re-drawing the assignment within blocks (M3 permutation test when unblocked) — exact under the sharp null whatever the serial correlation. |
| Assumptions | Carryover shorter than the burn-in; no anticipation; the burn-in fixed before the data (sensitivity reported, not used to choose). |
| Evaluation (results §9, 400 markets) | Bias by burn-in: 0 h −1.50 ± 0.08 (coverage 0.858), 1 h −0.48 ± 0.09, 2 h +0.04 ± 0.09 (coverage 0.955), 3 h +0.09 ± 0.10. A/A: within-block randomisation test 0.040; period-mean Welch under complete randomisation 0.050; **hourly Welch** 0.297 under complete randomisation and 0.003 under blocking — wrong in either direction. |
| Reference checks | With no noise, a burn-in covering the carryover recovers the global effect exactly; the within-block p-value matches full enumeration; the unblocked p-value equals the M3 permutation test (tests). |
| Failure modes | Carryover longer than the burn-in (bias toward zero); few periods (low power, coarse randomisation distribution); interference *between* periods beyond carryover (e.g. anticipation); periods too short for the outcome to respond. |

## 6. Data — `data/advanced_experiment_scenarios.py`

Additive; no M2 or M6 generator, table or contract changed.

* **Pre-period scenario**: spending propensity `λ_i` (log-normal); 28-day pre-period spend and
  14-day outcome are independent Gamma-noise measurements of it (pre/post correlation ≈ 0.62);
  heterogeneous revenue effect proportional to `λ_i` (mean exactly 0.6); conversion effect 0.01;
  10% new users without history; arrival over 28 days (sequential accrual). Planted invalid
  covariates: in-experiment sessions (raised by treatment) and a pre-period window that ends 7
  days after assignment.
* **Switchback market**: hourly orders with daily seasonality, weekend lift and AR(1) noise
  (φ = 0.6); direct effect 4, carryover 3 and 1.5 in the next two hours; global effect 8.5.
* **Clustered experiments** reuse the M6 generator (`n_clusters`, `cluster_sd`) unchanged.

Truths are computed from the generating process before assignment and read only after estimation.

## Known limitations

- Synthetic validation only; mechanisms (carryover length, pre/post correlation, ICC) are chosen to
  illustrate the methods and do not establish real-world magnitudes.
- Sequential methods assume approximately normal statistics with independent increments; delayed
  outcomes are handled only by analysing complete windows; novelty/primacy effects and
  time-varying effects violate the assumptions. No futility boundaries, no bias-adjusted estimates
  after early stopping, no information-based maximum-size re-estimation.
- The mSPRT uses a plug-in variance; its guarantee is asymptotic for binary or heavy-tailed data
  (simulated size 0.015, checked after every 1% of the M6 sample, is conservative).
- The normal-normal Bayesian model treats the SE as known; the Beta-binomial model assumes
  independent arm priors. Expected-loss decisions are only as good as the economics and the prior.
- Cluster planning uses a normal approximation with an effective sample size; very few clusters or
  strongly unequal sizes need simulation-based planning.
- Switchback analysis assumes carryover shorter than the burn-in and no anticipation; optimal
  period length and carryover-robust estimators (e.g. Bojinov–Simchi-Levi–Zhao designs) are not
  implemented.
- CUPED SEs ignore the estimation of θ (asymptotically negligible).
- Not implemented: geo experiments, interference/network designs, heterogeneous effects, bandits,
  long-term/surrogate effects, experiment-interaction detection, winner's-curse correction for
  A/B/n (carried from M6), exact binomial planning (carried from M3).
